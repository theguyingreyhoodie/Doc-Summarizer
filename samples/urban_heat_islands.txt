Urban Heat Islands and How Cities Are Fighting Back

Cities are measurably hotter than the countryside around them. On a summer afternoon, the centre of a large city can be 3 to 7 degrees Celsius warmer than nearby rural areas, and at night the gap can be even wider. Scientists call this the urban heat island effect, and it is becoming one of the most pressing public health problems of a warming planet.

The causes are well understood. Asphalt roads, dark rooftops and concrete walls absorb sunlight during the day and release that heat slowly after sunset. Tall buildings trap warm air and block the breezes that would otherwise carry it away. Vehicles, air conditioners and factories add waste heat of their own. Meanwhile, the trees and soil that cool rural land through evaporation have largely been paved over.

The consequences fall unevenly. Heat waves already kill more people in many countries than floods or storms, and the risk is highest for older adults, outdoor workers and people without air conditioning. Studies in several American and European cities have found that low-income neighbourhoods are often several degrees hotter than wealthier ones, largely because they have fewer trees and more pavement. High night-time temperatures are especially dangerous because the body never gets a chance to recover.

Heat islands also strain infrastructure. Electricity demand spikes as residents run air conditioners, which can overload power grids at the very moment they are needed most. Hotter surfaces speed up the formation of ground-level ozone, worsening air quality. Even stormwater is affected: rain that falls on scorching pavement can reach rivers several degrees warmer than normal, stressing fish and other aquatic life.

Cities are responding with a mix of old and new ideas. The simplest is planting trees. A mature tree can shade a street and cool the air around it through evapotranspiration, and large planting programmes in cities such as Melbourne and Medellín have measurably lowered local temperatures. Medellín's network of green corridors, built along roads and waterways, reduced temperatures along those routes by around two degrees.

Reflective surfaces are another tool. So-called cool roofs, coated with light-coloured or specially engineered materials, can reflect most incoming sunlight instead of absorbing it. Some cities now require cool roofs on new buildings, and Los Angeles has experimented with coating streets in a pale grey sealant that lowers surface temperatures. Researchers caution, however, that reflected light can warm nearby walls and pedestrians, so placement matters.

Water and design play a part too. Fountains, restored streams and permeable pavements that let rain soak into the ground all help cool their surroundings. Architects are rediscovering traditional techniques from hot climates, such as narrow shaded streets, courtyards and buildings oriented to catch prevailing winds. Green roofs and walls, covered in plants, combine insulation with evaporative cooling.

Finally, cities are planning for the heat they cannot prevent. Heat action plans set up cooling centres in libraries and community halls, send alerts to vulnerable residents and adjust working hours for outdoor labourers. Some cities have appointed chief heat officers to coordinate this work across departments.

No single measure will solve the problem, and as global temperatures rise the challenge will grow. But experts agree that combining greenery, smarter materials and better planning can make cities markedly cooler, healthier and fairer places to live.
