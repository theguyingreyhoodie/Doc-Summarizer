A Short History of the Internet

The internet did not begin as a single invention. It grew over several decades from research projects, academic networks and commercial services that gradually learned to speak a common language. Understanding that history helps explain both why the internet is so resilient and why it struggles with problems such as security and misinformation.

The story usually starts in the late 1950s. After the Soviet Union launched Sputnik in 1957, the United States created the Advanced Research Projects Agency, known as ARPA, to fund ambitious science and technology research. One of the problems ARPA researchers faced was that computers were expensive, rare and incompatible with one another. Scientists at different universities could not easily share machines, programs or data.

At the same time, engineers were exploring a new way to move information called packet switching. Paul Baran in the United States and Donald Davies in the United Kingdom independently proposed breaking messages into small blocks, or packets, that could travel separately across a network and be reassembled at their destination. Because packets could take different routes, the network had no single point of failure. If one link went down, traffic could simply flow around it.

These ideas came together in ARPANET. In October 1969, a computer at the University of California, Los Angeles sent the first message to a machine at the Stanford Research Institute. The plan was to type the word "login", but the system crashed after the first two letters, so the first message ever sent over the network was "lo". By the end of that year four sites were connected, and within a few years dozens of universities and research centres had joined.

As other networks appeared, a new problem emerged. Different networks used different rules, so they could not exchange data with one another. In 1974, Vinton Cerf and Robert Kahn published a design for a set of protocols that would let any network connect to any other. Their work became the Transmission Control Protocol and Internet Protocol, usually shortened to TCP/IP. TCP made sure data arrived complete and in the right order, while IP handled addressing and routing packets between networks.

On 1 January 1983, ARPANET officially switched to TCP/IP. Many historians treat that date as the birthday of the modern internet, because from then on separate networks could join a single, global "network of networks". The same year, the Domain Name System was designed, replacing long numeric addresses with memorable names. By the late 1980s, the National Science Foundation had built NSFNET, a faster backbone that connected universities across the United States and eventually replaced ARPANET entirely.

For most of this period, the internet was a tool for scientists and engineers. People used it for email, file transfers and discussion groups, but it required technical knowledge and was largely text-based. That changed because of a project at CERN, the European particle physics laboratory near Geneva. In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee proposed a system for sharing documents linked together by hypertext. By 1991 he had built the first web browser, the first web server and the first website, along with the standards that still underpin the web: HTML for writing pages, URLs for addressing them and HTTP for transferring them.

Crucially, CERN released the World Wide Web into the public domain in 1993, allowing anyone to use and build on it without paying fees. The same year, the Mosaic browser made the web graphical and easy to use, displaying images alongside text. Netscape Navigator followed in 1994, and the web began to grow at an astonishing rate. The number of websites rose from a few hundred in 1993 to millions by the end of the decade.

Commercialisation accelerated this growth. Restrictions on commercial traffic were lifted in the early 1990s, and in 1995 NSFNET was decommissioned, handing the backbone over to private providers. Companies such as Amazon and eBay were founded in the mid-1990s, search engines competed to organise the exploding volume of pages, and Google, founded in 1998, eventually came to dominate search. Investor enthusiasm fuelled the dot-com boom, which ended in a sharp crash in 2000 and 2001 when many unprofitable companies collapsed.

The internet nevertheless kept expanding. Broadband connections replaced slow dial-up modems in the early 2000s, making it practical to stream music and video. A new generation of services, often described as Web 2.0, let ordinary users publish content rather than simply read it. Blogs, Wikipedia, YouTube and social networks such as Facebook and Twitter turned the web into a participatory medium. The launch of the iPhone in 2007, followed by Android smartphones, moved the internet from the desk into people's pockets, and mobile devices soon became the main way many people went online.

This growth brought new challenges. The original protocols were designed for a small community of trusted researchers, so they included little protection against fraud, surveillance or attack. Security had to be added later through encryption, firewalls and authentication systems. The internet also ran short of addresses, because the original IP design allowed only about four billion of them, prompting the slow transition to IPv6, which provides vastly more. Questions about privacy, the power of large platforms and the spread of false information have become central political debates.

Today more than five billion people use the internet, and it underpins banking, commerce, education, government and entertainment. Yet its basic architecture still reflects the decisions made by its early designers: a decentralised network of networks, held together by open standards that no single company or country owns. That openness allowed the internet to grow from four computers in 1969 into the largest machine humanity has ever built, and it remains both its greatest strength and the source of many of its problems.
